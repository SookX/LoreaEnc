\begin{abstract}
We pretrain a compact 9M-parameter SqueezeFormer-XS encoder for English
ASR using a HuBERT-style masked-prediction objective over discrete units
derived directly from log-mel spectrogram chunks, requiring an estimated
\textbf{42 GPU-hours} of neural SSL optimization on 2$\times$NVIDIA H200
GPUs. First-stage targets are learned with
PCA+$K$-means at two granularities; a second stage re-clusters hidden
states from the iter-1 encoder. The iter-2 recipe reduces LibriSpeech
WER under greedy CTC decoding (no external language model) from
75.0/84.1\% to \textbf{34.1/51.5\%} on Libri-Light 10h
test-clean/test-other, and from 19.6/42.7\% to \textbf{16.1/36.2\%} on
the full train-clean-100. Against a faithful MelHuBERT-style baseline at
the same 9M-parameter scale, iter-2 reduces WER by \textbf{17--31\%
relative} across all 1h, 10h, and 100h cells; at 100h the
MelHuBERT-Transformer baseline collapses to roughly scratch
SqueezeFormer-XS performance, indicating that backbone and recipe choice
contribute complementary parts of the gap. We position the result
narrowly: this is not state-of-the-art ASR and not the first mel-input
SSL system; it is a controlled, matched-scale study showing that compact,
spectrogram-only SSL delivers large gains in low-label English ASR within
a two-GPU neural-optimization budget. Including iter-2 hidden-state extraction, the
full neural budget is about 48 GPU-hours, plus unlogged CPU preprocessing
for target generation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Related Work}
\label{sec:intro}

End-to-end ASR systems have become strong enough that the bottleneck in
many settings is no longer modeling capacity alone, but access to labeled
speech. Self-supervised pretraining addresses this by learning acoustic
representations from unlabeled audio before supervised CTC fine-tuning.
The dominant recipes operate on raw waveforms: wav2vec~2.0~\citep{wav2vec2}
learns contrastively over masked latent features, HuBERT~\citep{hubert}
predicts offline-clustered hidden units, WavLM~\citep{wavlm} adds
denoising and speaker-mixing objectives, and data2vec~\citep{data2vec}
distills teacher representations across modalities. These systems reach
strong LibriSpeech performance, but their standard recipes use large
encoders and thousands of reported GPU-hours. Multilingual and
weakly-supervised extensions --- XLS-R~\citep{xlsr}, MMS~\citep{mms},
Whisper~\citep{whisper} --- generalize across many languages but at
similar or greater compute and assume a released checkpoint covers the
target deployment. Our setting is complementary: single-corpus
pretraining of a compact recognizer in regimes where such checkpoints
may be unavailable or unsuitable.

Our choice of SqueezeFormer-XS~\citep{squeezeformer} as the sole
backbone is deliberate. In the original matched comparison,
SqueezeFormer-XS outperformed Conformer-CTC-S on LibriSpeech while
reducing FLOPs by 1.66$\times$. We therefore use it as a compact encoder
for studying whether spectrogram-domain SSL remains effective in the
sub-10M-parameter regime. This regime is not a trivial downscaling of
larger SSL encoders: a 9M-parameter model has much less capacity to
absorb noisy or overly fine discrete targets, so successful
dual-granularity pretraining is an empirical question rather than a
foregone conclusion.

A parallel line of work uses spectrograms directly. Masked Spectrogram
Prediction~\citep{msp}, AST~\citep{ast}, and SSAST~\citep{ssast} show
that masked objectives over mel-spectrogram patches can learn useful
audio representations. BEST-RQ~\citep{chiu2022self} is closer to ASR:
it predicts random-projection quantizer labels from acoustic features.
MelHuBERT~\citep{melhubert} is the closest related work to ours. It
simplifies HuBERT by feeding mel-spectrograms and reports favorable
performance while reducing pretraining time and MACs. We therefore do
not claim that mel-input SSL is new. Our question is narrower: under a
fixed 9M-parameter SqueezeFormer-XS~\citep{squeezeformer} ASR backbone,
can dual-granularity spectrogram targets improve low-label CTC
recognition?

Relative to MelHuBERT, our contribution is not the use of mel inputs or
stage-wise hidden-state refinement, both of which MelHuBERT already
studies. Instead, we focus on a different operating regime and target
design: a compact 9M-parameter SqueezeFormer-XS encoder trained with
dual-granularity discrete targets obtained from short log-mel chunks. We
evaluate this recipe under matched end-to-end CTC fine-tuning at 1h, 10h,
and 100h labeled budgets, including a direct head-to-head comparison
against a faithful MelHuBERT-style baseline at the same 9M-parameter
scale. In that comparison (\cref{tab:main}), our iter-2 recipe reduces
WER by 17--31\% relative across all six (subset $\times$ test-split)
cells; at 100h, the MelHuBERT-Transformer baseline collapses to roughly
scratch SqueezeFormer-XS performance, while iter-2 retains a substantial
gain.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Our pipeline has three stages: offline target generation, masked
prediction pretraining, and CTC fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Target generation.}
Let $\mathcal{D}=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N$ be the unlabeled corpus. For each
utterance we compute 80-bin log-mel features
$S_i=\phi(x_i)\in\mathbb{R}^{T_i\times d}$ and apply global CMVN
statistics estimated over $\mathcal{D}$. We then stack normalized frames
into chunks of size $c=8$ and stride $s=4$,
\begin{equation}
    C_i^{(t)}=\mathrm{vec}\!\left(\bar S_i[ts:ts+c]\right),
\end{equation}
choosing the stride to match the encoder output rate. Raw chunks are
high-dimensional, so we fit a whitened PCA projection to 64 dimensions on
sampled chunks and run MiniBatch $K$-means twice, with $K_c=100$ and
$K_f=500$. Each chunk is labeled by its nearest coarse and fine centroid:
\begin{align}
    z_i^{(c,t)} &= \arg\min_k \lVert \tilde C_i^{(t)}-v_k^{(c)} \rVert_2^2,\\
    z_i^{(f,t)} &= \arg\min_k \lVert \tilde C_i^{(t)}-v_k^{(f)} \rVert_2^2.
\end{align}
The coarse stream encourages broad acoustic categories, while the fine
stream preserves within-category variation.

\paragraph{Iter-2 refinement.}
For the second iteration, we run the iter-1 encoder over the same
unlabeled audio, collect hidden states at the target rate, and repeat the
same PCA+$K$-means procedure. The iter-2 encoder is initialized from
iter-1 and trained on these refined hidden-state targets. This adds one
encoder-forward target-extraction pass but no labeled data.

\paragraph{SSL objective.}
Given $\mathbf z_i^{(c)}$ and $\mathbf z_i^{(f)}$, we sample HuBERT-style
time masks, replace the corresponding input mel frames with a learnable
mask token, and train SqueezeFormer-XS $f_\theta$ to predict both target
streams at masked positions:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SSL}}(\theta)=
  \sum_{t:m_i^{(t)}=1}
    \mathrm{CE}(\hat z_i^{(c,t)},z_i^{(c,t)})
    +\mathrm{CE}(\hat z_i^{(f,t)},z_i^{(f,t)}).
  \label{eq:ssl-loss}
\end{equation}
Iter-1 uses 150k optimizer steps and iter-2 uses 100k steps. For ASR, we
discard the SSL heads, attach a BPE CTC projection, and fine-tune all
parameters on the labeled split. Scratch and SSL runs share the same
front-end, optimizer family, SpecAugment policy, tokenizer, and greedy
decoder; the encoder initialization is the only intended difference.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We evaluate on LibriSpeech~\citep{librispeech}. The labeled budgets are
Libri-Light 1h and 10h, plus the full \textit{train-clean-100} split.
The main comparison rows use SqueezeFormer-XS (16 blocks, hidden
dimension 144, $\sim$9M parameters) with a 128-piece SentencePiece BPE
tokenizer and greedy CTC decoding on \textit{test-clean} and
\textit{test-other}, with no external language model. SSL pretraining
uses HuBERT-style time masks (probability $p_m{=}0.30$, span
$\ell_m{=}10$ target steps). We report mean and sample standard
deviation over three fine-tuning seeds.

\paragraph{Main results.}
\cref{tab:main} shows that both SSL stages improve over scratch, and
iter-2 is best in all six cells. At 1h, iter-2 reduces test-clean WER
from 94.9\% to 56.8\% and test-other from 95.9\% to 68.7\%. These WERs
are still high, so the 1h result should be read as evidence that SSL
prevents the compact CTC model from failing outright, not as a deployable
recognizer. The largest absolute gain appears at 10h: test-clean drops
from 75.0\% to 34.1\%, and test-other from 84.1\% to 51.5\%. At 100h,
the gains are smaller but consistent, improving test-clean from 19.6\%
to 16.1\% and test-other from 42.7\% to 36.2\%.

The MelHuBERT-style row in \cref{tab:main} adds an external matched-scale
reference: a vanilla 9M-parameter Transformer encoder (faithful to the
architectural choice of the original MelHuBERT recipe), pretrained with a
single $K{=}500$ spectrogram codebook on the same 960h unlabeled corpus
and fine-tuned with the same per-budget CTC recipe and tokenizer. Iter-2
outperforms this baseline at every cell: by 21.3 / 17.4 WER points at 1h
(test-clean / test-other), 15.4 / 15.5 at 10h, and 4.6 / 7.3 at 100h ---
17--31\% relative reductions across the table. At 100h, the
MelHuBERT-Transformer baseline performs no better than scratch
SqueezeFormer-XS (20.7 vs 19.6\% test-clean; 43.5 vs 42.7\% test-other),
indicating that with sufficient labeled supervision the backbone choice
carries more of the iter-2 gap than the spectrogram-domain SSL
initialization. At 1h and 10h, by contrast, the SSL initialization
contributes the larger share of the gain.
The scratch baseline is intentionally strict. It uses the same 9M
SqueezeFormer-XS, the same mel front-end, and greedy decoding without an
LM. The per-budget CTC recipe (batch size, learning-rate schedule,
SpecAugment policy, epoch count) varies across 1h/10h/100h because the
optimal recipe at one scale is not appropriate at the others; within
each budget, the same recipe is applied identically to scratch, iter-1,
and iter-2, so the scratch row is not the result of using an
SSL-tuned recipe on a smaller split. The high WER in the 1h and 10h
conditions reflects the well-known failure mode of compact CTC-only
acoustic models on scarce labeled data: the network over-allocates the
blank token and under-emits words, so errors are deletion-dominated.
Adding an external language model, switching to an attention-based
decoder, or moving to a larger encoder would all likely lower absolute
WER, but each would change the matched-backbone, matched-decoder
comparison this paper is designed to isolate.

\paragraph{Target-choice ablation.}
\cref{tab:abl-codebook} compares single- and dual-codebook target
construction under the same SqueezeFormer-XS backbone, SSL budget, and
downstream CTC setup. This experiment is intended as an internal
ablation of our target design, not as a reproduction of MelHuBERT. The
dual-codebook objective consistently outperforms either single-codebook
variant at both fine-tuning budgets, suggesting that coarse and fine
cluster assignments provide complementary supervision. All three rows
use 50k SSL steps rather than the full iter-1 plus iter-2 schedule in
\cref{tab:main}; therefore, this ablation supports the target-design
choice under abbreviated training but does not by itself attribute the

\paragraph{Cluster sanity check.}
To verify that the spectrogram targets carry phonetic information, we
compute frame-level cluster purity and normalized mutual information
(NMI) against forced-aligned LibriSpeech phone labels. Over 86.1M aligned
chunks and 43 phone labels, the $K{=}100$ codebook reaches 28.4\% purity
(NMI 0.187), while the $K{=}500$ codebook reaches 32.0\% purity (NMI
0.192). These values are well above the chance level expected under
cluster--phone independence (NMI $\to 0$) and support the use of
spectrogram clusters as masked-prediction targets; purity is reported
alongside NMI because purity alone is sensitive to the prior of the
majority phone class.

\paragraph{Compute accounting.}
\cref{tab:compute} separates the reported compute components. The
headline 42 GPU-hours is not a fully measured end-to-end number; it is a
linear estimate for neural SSL optimization from a measured throughput of
8.4 wall-hours per 100k steps on 2$\times$NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Because H200
is substantially faster than the V100s used in the reported wav2vec~2.0
and HuBERT Base numbers, the cross-system GPU-hour ratio in
\cref{tab:abl-eff} overstates the algorithm-level efficiency advantage;
the absolute wall-clock budget on a single 2-GPU workstation remains the
operationally relevant quantity. As a rough hardware reference, H200
advertises on the order of 700--1000 dense FP16/BF16 Tensor-TFLOP/s
depending on form factor, versus about 125 FP16 Tensor-TFLOP/s for V100,
so the table should be read as a footprint comparison rather than a
same-hardware speedup claim. Iter-2
hidden-state extraction adds about 6 GPU-hours. Mel extraction, CMVN,
PCA, MiniBatch $K$-means, target assignment, and sharding were CPU
preprocessing jobs and were not separately timed in the original logs.
The scale gap to waveform-based systems in \cref{tab:abl-eff} reflects
three compounding factors --- a smaller encoder (9M vs 95M+), fewer
total optimizer steps (250k vs 400k--650k), and lower per-step
input-processing cost from operating on mel features rather than raw
waveforms --- and we do not separately isolate which dominates. The
original training configurations of those systems used many more GPUs
than would be necessary today; the GPU-hour column reflects what was
reported, and is meant to contextualize scale rather than to claim
algorithm-level efficiency at matched hardware.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main finding is that compact ASR can benefit substantially from
HuBERT-style masked prediction even when the pretraining targets come
from spectrogram chunks rather than a waveform transformer. The iter-2
improvement is also important: re-clustering encoder hidden states gives
more useful targets than the first-pass log-mel chunks alone, consistent
with the refinement logic in HuBERT. This stage-wise refinement is also
consistent with MelHuBERT; our distinct empirical question is whether a
dual-granularity target design remains beneficial for a much smaller
SqueezeFormer-XS encoder.

\paragraph{What carries the gap to MelHuBERT.}
The cross-architecture comparison in \cref{tab:main} factors the
iter-2 vs MelHuBERT gap by labeled-data regime in a way the
within-codebase ablation (\cref{tab:abl-codebook}, single- vs
dual-codebook under a fixed SqueezeFormer-XS backbone) cannot. At 1h
and 10h, MelHuBERT-Transformer is clearly better than scratch, and the
remaining 15--21 WER-point gap to iter-2 reflects the joint effect of
the dual-codebook target design and the SqueezeFormer-XS backbone. At
100h, by contrast, MelHuBERT-Transformer is statistically
indistinguishable from scratch SqueezeFormer-XS, and the entire
4--7-point iter-2 advantage at that budget is attributable to
dual-codebook SSL on top of the better backbone. The two contributions
are therefore complementary rather than redundant: SSL initialization
dominates the gap in the low-resource regime, and the
ASR-specialised backbone dominates it once supervision is plentiful.

\paragraph{On fine-tuning a released wav2vec~2.0 / HuBERT checkpoint.}
A natural alternative baseline is to fine-tune a public wav2vec~2.0 or
HuBERT checkpoint on the same 1h--100h splits. We do not include such a
row in \cref{tab:main} because it does not fit the matched-backbone
question this paper asks: keeping the released 95M waveform encoder
changes the parameter regime, while distilling it into the 9M
SqueezeFormer-XS would add a separate optimization stage and its own
compute footprint. We therefore treat fine-tuned-from-released-checkpoint
systems as out-of-regime references rather than headline baselines,
while acknowledging that for languages where strong released checkpoints
already exist, that route may simply be the better practical choice.

The scope is limited. All experiments are English LibriSpeech; we do not
claim multilingual performance. A deliberate end-to-end validation on
Bulgarian is in preparation as a non-English extension of this proof of
concept. Because multilingual released systems such as MMS~\citep{mms}
already support Bulgarian, that study will evaluate whether compact
per-language pretraining offers a useful compute/accuracy trade-off
relative to adapting a large multilingual checkpoint. The backbone is a
single 9M-parameter SqueezeFormer-XS model.
All WERs use greedy CTC decoding without an external language model;
$n$-gram LM rescoring or shallow fusion would likely lower absolute
numbers, particularly in the 1h and 10h conditions where the acoustic
model under-emits, but this is orthogonal to the pretraining contribution
evaluated here. The single-codebook comparison in
\cref{tab:abl-codebook} is an internal spectrogram-HuBERT-style ablation
under our codebase, not a full reproduction of MelHuBERT at its original
scale. Finally, the compute comparison to wav2vec~2.0 and HuBERT uses
published figures for those systems and our measured/estimated accounting
in \cref{tab:compute}; a same-hardware comparison would be needed for a
stronger efficiency claim.

\paragraph{Outlook.} We view the present paper as Step~1 of a small
research program targeting compute-frugal compact ASR.
\textbf{Step~1 (this work):} establish that spectrogram-only SSL is
viable at $\sim$42 GPU-hours on a 9M-parameter SqueezeFormer-XS, on
English LibriSpeech, with monotone scratch $<$ iter-1 $<$ iter-2 across
all three labeled budgets.
\textbf{Step~2:} run the same recipe across SqueezeFormer-M/-L to test
whether iter-2 gains scale with encoder capacity, and obtain controlled
WER comparisons against MelHuBERT and wav2vec~2.0 / HuBERT at matched
fine-tuning budgets.
\textbf{Step~3:} use the larger SSL-pretrained variants as teachers for
knowledge distillation back into SqueezeFormer-XS, and validate the full
pipeline on Bulgarian as a representative Slavic-language case study,
including comparison against an available multilingual baseline such as
MMS. The intended deliverable is a per-language pretraining pipeline
whose neural SSL stage remains reproducible on a two-GPU
workstation-class setup.

\begin{abstract}
We study spectrogram-based self-supervised pretraining for compact
low-label automatic speech recognition. Our model uses a 9M-parameter
SqueezeFormer-XS encoder trained with a HuBERT-style masked-prediction
objective over dual-granularity discrete targets derived from log-mel
chunks, followed by a second stage that re-clusters encoder hidden
states. On LibriSpeech with greedy CTC decoding and no external language
model, iter-2 pretraining reduces test-clean/test-other WER from
75.0/84.1 to 34.1/51.5 in the 10h setting and from 19.6/42.7 to
16.1/36.2 on train-clean-100. The same monotonic scratch
$<$ iter-1 $<$ iter-2 trend holds in the 1h setting as well. The reported
training run requires approximately 42 H200 GPU-hours for SSL
optimization and approximately 48 H200 GPU-hours including iter-2 target
extraction. These results show that spectrogram-based SSL improves a compact
ASR encoder across all tested label budgets at a training cost below 50
H200 GPU-hours for neural SSL optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Related Work}
\label{sec:intro}


End-to-end ASR remains difficult with limited labeled speech,
especially for compact encoders where scale is not an option.
Self-supervised learning (SSL) addresses this by learning acoustic
representations from unlabeled audio before supervised CTC
fine-tuning, but many successful systems use substantially larger
encoders than are convenient for small-model studies. We ask a
narrower question: can spectrogram-based HuBERT-style pretraining
improve a 9M-parameter SqueezeFormer-XS~\citep{squeezeformer}
recognizer under limited labeled supervision?
\citet{squeezeformer} report consistently superior
accuracy--parameter tradeoffs over Conformer~\citep{conformer}
across model sizes, making SqueezeFormer-XS the natural
choice for this study.

Spectrogram-based SSL offers a way to reduce dependence on labeled
data without adding inference cost. Our setting therefore tests
whether spectrogram-based masked-prediction pretraining provides
consistent gains when the downstream recognizer is restricted to a
sub-10M-parameter model.

Prior SSL systems such as wav2vec~2.0~\citep{wav2vec2} and
HuBERT~\citep{hubert} established masked-prediction pretraining for
ASR, while MelHuBERT~\citep{melhubert} showed that similar objectives
can operate directly on mel-spectrograms with hidden-state target
refinement. Building on this line of work, we apply spectrogram-based
dual-codebook masked prediction to a compact 9M-parameter
SqueezeFormer-XS encoder and evaluate its effectiveness across 1h,
10h, and 100h labeled-data regimes. We do not claim a new SSL
formulation, but the combination of dual-granularity targets and
transparent compute accounting under limited supervision is the focus
of this study.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
First, we evaluate two-stage spectrogram-domain SSL for a compact
9M-parameter SqueezeFormer-XS encoder across 1h, 10h, and 100h
labeled-data regimes. Second, we show consistent WER improvements from
scratch to iter-1 to iter-2 under matched SqueezeFormer fine-tuning.
Third, we report the GPU-side training budget of the resulting compact
SSL pipeline and provide a matched-backbone abbreviated ablation of
dual-codebook targets.

\section{Method and Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Target generation.}
Let $\mathcal{D}=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N$ be the unlabeled corpus. For each
utterance we compute 80-bin log-mel features
$S_i=\phi(x_i)\in\mathbb{R}^{T_i\times d}$ and apply global CMVN
statistics estimated over $\mathcal{D}$. We then stack normalized frames
into chunks of size $c=8$ and stride $s=4$,
\begin{equation}
    C_i^{(t)}=\mathrm{vec}\!\left(\bar S_i[ts:ts+c]\right),
\end{equation}
choosing the stride to match the encoder output rate. Raw chunks are
high-dimensional, so we fit a whitened PCA projection to 64 dimensions on
sampled chunks and run MiniBatch $K$-means twice, with $K_c=100$ and
$K_f=500$. Each PCA-projected chunk is assigned independently to its
nearest centroid in the $K{=}100$ and $K{=}500$ codebooks. We use two
codebooks at different granularities to expose the masked-prediction
objective to complementary coarse and fine assignments; \cref{tab:abl-codebook}
evaluates this design under matched abbreviated pretraining.

\paragraph{Iter-2 refinement.}
For the second iteration, we run the iter-1 encoder over the same
unlabeled audio, collect hidden states at the target rate, and repeat the
same PCA+$K$-means procedure. The iter-2 encoder is initialized from
iter-1 and trained on these refined hidden-state targets. This adds one
encoder-forward target-extraction pass but no labeled data.

Iter-1 and iter-2 share the same masked-prediction structure but differ
in the targets being predicted: iter-1 uses clusters derived directly
from local log-mel chunks, while iter-2 uses clusters derived from
representations learned by the iter-1 encoder. This comparison evaluates
whether representation-based target refinement provides additional
benefit over the initial acoustic-target stage within the same compact
pipeline.

\paragraph{SSL objective and CTC fine-tuning.}
Given $\mathbf z_i^{(c)}$ and $\mathbf z_i^{(f)}$, we sample HuBERT-style
time masks, replace the corresponding input mel frames with a learnable
mask token, and train SqueezeFormer-XS $f_\theta$ to predict both target
streams at masked positions:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SSL}}(\theta)=
  \sum_{t:m_i^{(t)}=1}
    \mathrm{CE}(\hat z_i^{(c,t)},z_i^{(c,t)})
    +\mathrm{CE}(\hat z_i^{(f,t)},z_i^{(f,t)}).
  \label{eq:ssl-loss}
\end{equation}
Iter-1 uses 150k optimizer steps and iter-2 uses 100k steps. For ASR, we
discard the SSL heads, attach a BPE CTC projection, and fine-tune all
parameters on the labeled split. Scratch and SSL runs share the same
front-end, optimizer family, SpecAugment policy, tokenizer, and greedy
decoder; the encoder initialization is the only intended difference.

\paragraph{Experimental protocol.}
We evaluate on LibriSpeech~\citep{librispeech}. The labeled budgets are
Libri-Light 1h and 10h, plus the full \textit{train-clean-100} split.
The main SqueezeFormer rows use a 16-block SqueezeFormer-XS encoder with
hidden dimension 144 ($\sim$9M parameters), an 80-bin mel front-end, a
128-piece SentencePiece BPE tokenizer, and greedy CTC decoding on
\textit{test-clean} and \textit{test-other} with no external language
model. SSL pretraining uses the 960h unlabeled training corpus and
HuBERT-style time masks with probability $p_m{=}0.30$ and span
$\ell_m{=}10$ target steps. We report mean and sample standard deviation
over three fine-tuning seeds.

\subsection{Main Results}
\cref{tab:main} shows that for the same SqueezeFormer-XS encoder
and CTC fine-tuning recipe, both SSL stages improve over scratch,
and iter-2 is best in every labeled-data regime. The largest
absolute gain appears at 10h, where test-clean/test-other WER drops
from 75.0/84.1 to 34.1/51.5. At 1h, iter-2 reduces WER from
94.9/95.9 to 56.8/68.7. These numbers remain high, as expected for
a 9M-parameter CTC model without a language model at this data
scale, but the absolute reductions show that SSL substantially
changes what the compact encoder learns from scarce labels. At
100h, the gains are smaller but consistent, improving from
19.6/42.7 to 16.1/36.2.

The scratch rows use the same fine-tuning setup as the SSL rows
rather than a separately tuned configuration. Their very high
1h/10h greedy-CTC WERs therefore indicate the instability of this
compact supervised baseline under scarce labels, not a claim about
the best supervised compact ASR result.

Relative to scratch, iter-2 reduces test-clean/test-other WER by
40.1\%/28.4\% at 1h, 54.5\%/38.8\% at 10h, and 17.9\%/15.2\% at 100h.
The greatest relative gain occurs at 10h.

Target refinement also helps consistently across all label budgets.
Relative to iter-1, iter-2 lowers test-clean/test-other WER by
6.7/6.4 points at 1h, 4.8/6.0 points at 10h, and 1.1/2.3 points
at 100h. The gain is largest where labels are scarcest, suggesting
that better target structure matters most when the supervised signal
is weak.

The MelHuBERT-inspired Transformer row in \cref{tab:main} is an
external reference point rather than a direct comparison. It matches
parameter scale, tokenizer, greedy decoding, unlabeled corpus, and
downstream label splits, but differs in encoder architecture and
target configuration. Across all six cells, iter-2 is lower by
4.6--21.3 absolute WER points, corresponding to 17--31\% relative
reductions.

\subsection{Target-Choice Ablation}
\cref{tab:abl-codebook} evaluates target choice independently of the
full two-stage schedule by holding the SqueezeFormer-XS backbone and
the 50k-step pretraining budget fixed. The $K{=}500$ target set
improves over $K{=}100$, and dual-codebook targets improve further
in all four cells. Relative to $K{=}500$, the dual objective reduces
WER by 1.5/1.0 points at 10h and 0.5/0.5 points at 100h on
test-clean/test-other. These modest but consistent gains support
dual-target supervision under this shortened training run, though
they do not explain the larger final iter-2 improvements in
\cref{tab:main}. Whether the dual-codebook margin widens at full
pretraining length is an open question. The shortened schedule is
sufficient to validate the codebook design choice but not to
attribute the final iter-2 gains to dual codebooks alone.

\subsection{Cluster Sanity Check}
Against forced-aligned LibriSpeech phones, the $K{=}100$ codebook reaches
28.4\% purity and 0.187 NMI over 86.1M aligned chunks, while the
$K{=}500$ codebook reaches 32.0\% purity and 0.192 NMI. This provides a
light sanity check that the spectrogram clusters contain non-trivial
phonetic information.

\subsection{Compute Accounting}
\label{sec:compute}

\cref{tab:compute} breaks down the reported training cost by stage.
The 42 GPU-hours covers optimizer steps only and is a linear estimate
from a measured throughput of 8.4 wall-hours per 100k steps on
2$\times$NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Adding the iter-2 forward pass for target
re-extraction brings the total to approximately 48 H200 GPU-hours.
Both training stages together correspond to approximately 0.168
GPU-hours per 1,000 optimizer updates, and the re-extraction pass
accounts for 12.5\% of that total. Mel extraction, CMVN, PCA,
MiniBatch $K$-means, target assignment, and sharding ran on CPU and
were not separately timed.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Fine-tuning a released wav2vec~2.0 or HuBERT checkpoint on the same
labeled splits would change the encoder from 9M to 95M+ parameters,
which is a different experimental regime entirely. We therefore treat
such systems as out-of-scope references rather than baselines for
this study.

These results establish a compact English proof of concept: under a
fixed SqueezeFormer-XS fine-tuning setup, spectrogram-based SSL
provides consistent gains at every label budget, and re-clustering
encoder hidden states in the second pretraining stage improves over
the initial log-mel targets. The codebook ablation supports using
dual targets under shortened pretraining, while the MelHuBERT-inspired
Transformer row provides a reference point at comparable parameter
scale without isolating architecture from target choice.

The scope remains limited. All experiments use English LibriSpeech as
a controlled proof-of-concept corpus. The pipeline is language-agnostic
by construction, requiring only a log-mel front-end, $K$-means over the
target corpus, and a BPE tokenizer fitted to the target language.
Extension to low-resource languages with no existing pretrained SSL
encoder is the primary intended application of future work. All WERs
use greedy CTC decoding without an external language model. Stronger
decoders or language-model rescoring would likely change absolute WER
but leave the pretraining comparison intact. The single-codebook
comparison is a shortened ablation under our codebase rather than a
full reproduction of MelHuBERT at its original scale. Finally, the reported 42 GPU-hours covers H200 optimizer steps
only, while the 48 GPU-hour total additionally includes iter-2
target extraction. CPU preprocessing was not timed.

\paragraph{Outlook.}
We view this as the first step of a larger program. The next step
validates the same pipeline across SqueezeFormer-M, -L, and -XL to
establish whether gains scale with encoder capacity and to enable
direct WER comparisons against wav2vec~2.0 and HuBERT at matched
supervision budgets. The final step uses the larger SSL-pretrained
variants as teachers for knowledge distillation back into
SqueezeFormer-XS, targeting languages where labeled data is scarce
but unlabeled audio is available. This is precisely the setting where
SSL is most needed and existing large pretrained checkpoints are
least likely to cover the target language. Every stage is designed
to run on two GPUs, keeping the full program within reach of
research groups without institutional compute.
